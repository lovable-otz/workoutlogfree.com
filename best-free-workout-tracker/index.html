Best Free Workout Trackers

The best free workout tracker app is whichever one you will still be filling in six months from now, and that almost always comes down to logging speed, not features. Before you commit a year of training data, check three things: how fast you can record a set, whether your history exports, and what happens when the free tier changes.

What matters in a tracker

Feature lists are the wrong way to choose a tracker. Almost every option can store a set; what separates them is friction, ownership and what happens on the day the pricing changes. These are the criteria worth testing before you commit, with the question to ask of each.

CriterionThe testWhy it decides the outcome
Logging speedTime yourself entering three sets of fiveThe single strongest predictor of whether you keep using it
Repeat-set entryCan you copy the previous set in one tap?Straight sets are most of lifting; retyping them kills logs
Progress viewCan you see one exercise over months, not one session?The whole reason to log is to compare across weeks
ExportCan you download a CSV today, without paying?Export is the only real guarantee you can leave later
OfflineTurn on airplane mode in the gym and log a setBasements have no signal
Account requiredCan you use it before handing over an email?An account is what turns your log into someone else's asset
Free tier limitsWhat is capped: routines, history length, charts?Caps usually bite months in, once switching is painful
Ads in the flowDoes an ad interrupt between sets?Interruptions during rest periods are the main reason people quit an app

Logging speed deserves the emphasis. A five-exercise session is roughly fifteen to twenty sets. If an app takes eight seconds per set instead of three, that is over a minute of extra phone time every session, spent standing between working sets. It is a small cost that compounds into abandonment.

Export deserves the second-loudest mention, because it is the one thing you cannot add later. A tracker with a plain CSV export is reversible: if it gets worse, or gets bought, or starts charging, you leave with everything. A tracker whose export sits behind a subscription has effectively priced your own history, and the price goes up as the history grows.

Two criteria matter less than the marketing suggests. Exercise libraries with animations are pleasant and irrelevant once you know your six or eight movements. Automatic plate calculators and rest timers are conveniences, not reasons to choose a platform. Neither affects whether you can answer the only question that matters in the gym: what did I lift for this last time, and can I beat it today?

Read the table by column, not by row. If cross-device sync is non-negotiable because you train at two gyms and switch phones, the browser log and the notebook are out, and everything else in the row matters less. If you want no account under any circumstances, the field narrows to paper and browser storage, and you accept the sync loss.

The watch row is worth spelling out, because it is where expectations most often break. Wearables are strong at the things they can measure without you: elapsed time, heart rate, distance, pace. None of them reliably knows that the bar held 82.5 kg or that you got seven reps rather than eight. Every lifting log ends in manual entry, whatever the device on your wrist promises.

The spreadsheet row is also more competitive than it looks. A spreadsheet is free, exportable by definition, and syncs. Its weakness is entry on a phone, which is exactly the moment that matters: typing into a small grid between sets with cold hands is slower than any purpose-built form. Plenty of people solve this by logging on paper in the gym and typing it up later, which is a perfectly good system.

If you want the criteria applied to a specific app, apply them yourself: install it, log one real session, then try to export the file. Twenty minutes of that tells you more than any ranked list.

When a browser log is enough

A browser log covers a specific and fairly common case: you lift a familiar set of movements, you train on one device, and you want the numbers without signing up for anything. If that describes you, the tool on this site is enough, and adding an app would mostly add notifications.

It fits particularly well in three situations. First, starting out, when you do not yet know whether the habit will stick and do not want to evaluate software before your third session. Second, coming back after a break, when the job is simply to write down what you did today so that next week has a number to beat. Third, when you deliberately do not want a fitness account: no email, no profile, no history sitting on a server you cannot inspect.

The honest limits are worth stating just as plainly. Data lives in this browser's localStorage. Clear your site data and it is gone. Open the page on a different phone and the history is not there, because there is nothing to sync from. Private browsing windows forget everything on close. The mitigation is one button: export a CSV whenever you would mind losing the record, and keep the file somewhere you back up.

A browser log also pairs well with the thing it cannot replace. General activity, walking and cardio are tracked well enough by the phone you already carry. The CDC physical activity guidelines for adults put the adult target at 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on 2 or more days a week. Your phone can answer the first half without being asked; the second half is what you type into a log.

Start with the free workout tracker and the planner, and if you outgrow them, you leave with a CSV rather than a negotiation. That is the part most free tiers do not offer.
